\hypertarget{the-apparatus-binets-orbit-equation}{%
\subsection{§1. The apparatus --- Binet's orbit
equation}\label{the-apparatus-binets-orbit-equation}}

We consider a particle of mass \texttt{m} in a central force
\texttt{F(r)\ ê\_r}, with the conserved angular momentum per unit mass

\[
L \;=\; r^{2}\,\dot\phi. \tag{2}
\]

Following Binet, we change variables to \texttt{u\ =\ 1/r}. The orbit
equation in \texttt{u(φ)} form is

\[
\frac{d^{2} u}{d\phi^{2}} \;+\; u
\;=\; -\,\frac{1}{m\,L^{2}\,u^{2}}\,F\!\left(\tfrac{1}{u}\right). \tag{3}
\]

This is the standard apparatus for perihelion-advance
calculations\footnote{H. Goldstein, C. Poole, and J. Safko,
  \emph{Classical Mechanics}, 3rd ed. (Addison-Wesley, 2002), §3.5; C.
  W. Misner, K. S. Thorne, and J. A. Wheeler, \emph{Gravitation}
  (Freeman, 1973), Box 25.6; R. M. Wald, \emph{General Relativity}
  (University of Chicago Press, 1984), §6.3.}. Its advantage is that,
for an inverse-square force, the equation is linear in \texttt{u} with a
constant right-hand side. It then solves exactly, and the geometry of
the orbit is read off directly from the solution. Any correction to
Newton's force law shows up as an extra term on the right of (3). The
perihelion-advance question reduces, in each case, to a single technical
question: what does that extra term do to the periodicity in \texttt{φ}
of the solution?

\begin{center}\rule{0.5\linewidth}{0.5pt}\end{center}

\hypertarget{the-unperturbed-orbit-closed-ellipse-from-newtons--gmruxb2}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{§2. The unperturbed orbit --- closed ellipse
from Newton's
\texttt{-GM/r²}}{§2. The unperturbed orbit --- closed ellipse from Newton's -GM/r²}}\label{the-unperturbed-orbit-closed-ellipse-from-newtons--gmruxb2}}

For Newton, \texttt{F(r)\ =\ -GMm/r²}, so that
\texttt{F(1/u)\ =\ -GMm\ u²}. Substituting into (3),

\[
\frac{d^{2} u}{d\phi^{2}} \;+\; u
\;=\; \frac{GM}{L^{2}}. \tag{4}
\]

Equation (4) is harmonic-oscillator-with-constant-forcing. Its general
solution is

\[
u(\phi) \;=\; \frac{1}{p}\,\bigl[\,1 + e\cos(\phi - \phi_{0})\,\bigr],
\qquad
p \;\equiv\; \frac{L^{2}}{GM}. \tag{5}
\]

The quantity \texttt{p} is the semi-latus rectum. We verify the solution
by substituting (5) into (4); the residual
\texttt{d²u/dφ²\ +\ u\ −\ GM/L²} simplifies to \texttt{0} in
Wolfram\footnote{Wolfram MCP verification: substitute
  \texttt{u(φ)\ =\ (1\ +\ e\ cos\ φ)/p} with \texttt{p\ =\ L²/GM} into
  the unperturbed equation; the residual
  \texttt{d²u/dφ²\ +\ u\ −\ GM/L²} simplifies to \texttt{0}.}.

The function \texttt{u(φ)} has period \texttt{2π}. Perihelion occurs
when \texttt{u} is maximal, that is, when \texttt{cos(φ\ −\ φ₀)\ =\ 1},
which recurs every \texttt{2π} in \texttt{φ}. Thus the orbit is a closed
ellipse and the perihelion does not advance:

\[
\Delta\phi^{\text{Newton}}_{\text{per orbit}} \;=\; 0. \tag{6}
\]

We observe that this is Bertrand's theorem in a familiar guise: of all
central potentials, only Newton's \texttt{1/r} and the harmonic
\texttt{r²} give universally closed bound orbits.

\hypertarget{the-original-method-standard-gr-via-schwarzschild}{%
\subsection{§3. The original method --- standard GR via
Schwarzschild}\label{the-original-method-standard-gr-via-schwarzschild}}

In Schwarzschild geometry, the geodesic equation for a test particle in
the equatorial plane reduces to a Binet equation with one extra
term\footnote{Misner, Thorne, and Wheeler, \emph{Gravitation}, Box 25.6
  and Eq. (25.40); the equation appears here as (7).}:

\[
\frac{d^{2} u}{d\phi^{2}} \;+\; u
\;=\; \frac{GM}{L^{2}}
\;+\; \frac{3\,GM}{c^{2}}\,u^{2}. \tag{7}
\]

The new term \texttt{3GM\ u²/c²} is the relativistic perturbation. We
work to first order in the small parameter

\[
\epsilon \;\equiv\; \frac{GM}{c^{2}\,p}, \tag{8}
\]

and write \texttt{u\ =\ u₀\ +\ δu}, with \texttt{u₀} the unperturbed
Newton solution from §2.

The perturbation source on the right of (7) is

\[
\frac{3\,GM}{c^{2}}\,u_{0}^{2}
\;=\; \frac{3\,GM}{c^{2}\,p^{2}}\,\bigl(1 + e\cos\phi\bigr)^{2}
\;=\; \frac{3\,GM}{c^{2}\,p^{2}}\,\Bigl[\,1 + 2e\cos\phi + e^{2}\cos^{2}\phi\,\Bigr]. \tag{9}
\]

We expand (9) symbolically; the coefficient of \texttt{cos\ φ} is
\texttt{6\ GM\ e/(c²\ p²)}, and this is the load-bearing
resonant-driving term\footnote{Wolfram MCP verification:
  \texttt{3GM/c²\ ·\ u₀²} expanded symbolically gives a coefficient of
  \texttt{cos\ φ} equal to \texttt{6\ GM\ e/(c²\ p²)}.}.

It follows that the \texttt{cos\ φ} piece of the perturbation resonantly
drives the homogeneous equation

\[
\frac{d^{2}(\delta u)}{d\phi^{2}} \;+\; \delta u \;=\; \frac{6\,GM\,e}{c^{2}\,p^{2}}\,\cos\phi, \tag{10}
\]

producing a secular term

\[
\delta u_{\text{secular}}(\phi)
\;=\; \frac{3\,GM\,e}{c^{2}\,p^{2}}\,\phi\,\sin\phi. \tag{11}
\]

The other pieces of (9) --- the constant \texttt{1\ +\ e²/2} piece and
the \texttt{cos\ 2φ} piece arising from \texttt{e²cos²φ/2} --- give
bounded, non-secular corrections; they do not shift the perihelion.

Adding (11) to \texttt{u₀},

\[
u(\phi) \;\approx\; \frac{1}{p}\,\Bigl[\,1 + e\cos\phi
\;+\; \frac{3\,GM}{c^{2}\,p}\,e\,\phi\,\sin\phi\,\Bigr]
\;=\; \frac{1}{p}\,\Bigl[\,1 + e\cos\bigl(\phi(1-k)\bigr)\,\Bigr]
\;+\; \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}), \tag{12}
\]

with

\[
k \;=\; \frac{3\,GM}{c^{2}\,p}. \tag{13}
\]

The trigonometric identification used in (12) is the small-\texttt{k}
expansion

\[
\cos\bigl(\phi\,(1 - k)\bigr) \;\approx\; \cos\phi \;+\; k\,\phi\,\sin\phi. \tag{13a}
\]

Perihelion occurs at \texttt{φ(1\ −\ k)\ =\ 2πn}. Successive perihelia
are therefore separated by
\texttt{φ\ =\ 2π/(1\ −\ k)\ ≈\ 2π(1\ +\ k)\ =\ 2π\ +\ 2π\ k}, and the
advance per orbit is

\[
\Delta\phi^{\text{GR}}_{\text{per orbit}}
\;=\; 2\pi k
\;=\; \frac{6\pi\,GM}{c^{2}\,p}
\;=\; \frac{6\pi\,GM}{c^{2}\,a\,(1-e^{2})}. \tag{14}
\]

This is the canonical Einstein 1915 result. For Mercury we use the
standard orbital parameters: semi-major axis
\texttt{a\ =\ 5.7909\ ×\ 10¹⁰} m, eccentricity \texttt{e\ =\ 0.20563},
and semi-latus rectum \texttt{p\ =\ a(1\ −\ e²)\ =\ 5.546\ ×\ 10¹⁰} m.
Indeed, evaluating (14) at these parameters,

\[
\Delta\phi^{\text{GR}}_{\text{Mercury}}
\;=\; \frac{6\pi\,GM_{\odot}}{c^{2}\,p}
\;\times\; \frac{100 \cdot 365.25 \cdot 86400}{T_{\text{orb}}}
\;\times\; \frac{180 \cdot 3600}{\pi}
\;\text{arcsec/century}
\;=\; 42.99\;\text{arcsec/century}. \tag{15}
\]

The numerical evaluation is performed in Wolfram\footnote{Wolfram MCP
  verification: with \texttt{GM\_⊙\ =\ 1.327\ ×\ 10²⁰},
  \texttt{c\ =\ 2.998\ ×\ 10⁸}, \texttt{p\ =\ 5.546\ ×\ 10¹⁰},
  \texttt{T\ =\ 87.969\ ·\ 86400}, the expression
  \texttt{6π\ GM/(c²\ p)\ ·\ 415.2\ ·\ (180\ ·\ 3600/π)} evaluates to
  \texttt{42.9918} arcsec/c.} with \texttt{GM\_⊙\ =\ 1.327\ ×\ 10²⁰},
\texttt{c\ =\ 2.998\ ×\ 10⁸}, \texttt{p\ =\ 5.546\ ×\ 10¹⁰}, and
\texttt{T\ =\ 87.969\ ·\ 86400}. The result \texttt{42.9918}
arcsec/century is the canonical figure.

\hypertarget{the-proper-time-update-relativistic-newtonian-theory-force-law}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{§4. The proper-time update ---
\emph{Relativistic Newtonian Theory} force
law}{§4. The proper-time update --- Relativistic Newtonian Theory force law}}\label{the-proper-time-update-relativistic-newtonian-theory-force-law}}

The framework's modified Newton force, derived\footnote{Pull request
  analysis:
  \texttt{Roadmapping/Equation\_Verification/Dual\_Newtonian\_Theory.md},
  linked from pull request \#83,
  \texttt{github.com/temoTxt/PyPhysics/pull/83}. That document verifies
  (17) via Wolfram MCP from the proper-time Hamiltonian (16).} from the
canonical proper-time Hamiltonian

\[
K \;=\; \frac{\boldsymbol\pi^{2}}{2m} \;+\; m c^{2} \;+\; V \;+\; \frac{V^{2}}{2 m c^{2}}, \tag{16}
\]

with \texttt{V\ =\ -GMm/r}, takes the form

\[
\vec{a}_{m}
\;=\; -\,\frac{GM}{r^{2}}\,\Bigl(1 \;-\; \frac{GM}{c^{2}\,r}\Bigr)\,\hat{u}_{r}. \tag{17}
\]

We refer to (17) as the framework force law; it is the equation labelled
(h4) in Gill's paper. Multiplying by \texttt{m} gives the force itself,

\[
F(r) \;=\; -\,\frac{GM\,m}{r^{2}} \;+\; \frac{(GM)^{2}\,m}{c^{2}\,r^{3}}. \tag{18}
\]

Substituting (18) into Binet's equation (3), and writing \texttt{L} for
the angular momentum per unit mass,

\[
\frac{d^{2} u}{d\phi^{2}} \;+\; u
\;=\; -\,\frac{1}{L^{2}\,u^{2}}\!\left[\,-\,GM\,u^{2} \;+\; \frac{(GM)^{2}}{c^{2}}\,u^{3}\,\right]
\;=\; \frac{GM}{L^{2}} \;-\; \frac{(GM)^{2}}{c^{2}\,L^{2}}\,u. \tag{19}
\]

Rearranging (19),

\[
\frac{d^{2} u}{d\phi^{2}}
\;+\; u\,\underbrace{\Bigl[\,1 + \frac{(GM)^{2}}{c^{2}\,L^{2}}\,\Bigr]}_{\equiv\,\alpha^{2}}
\;=\; \frac{GM}{L^{2}}. \tag{20}
\]

We observe the structural difference between (20) and the Schwarzschild
equation (7). The Schwarzschild perturbation \texttt{3GM\ u²/c²} is
quadratic in \texttt{u} and drives a resonant secular term. The
framework's perturbation \texttt{−(GM)²\ u/(c²\ L²)} is linear in
\texttt{u}. It is therefore absorbed into the homogeneous part as a
shift of the effective spring constant from \texttt{1} to \texttt{α²}.
The two perturbations have completely different structure.

Equation (20) is again a harmonic-oscillator-with-constant-forcing
problem, with frequency \texttt{α} in place of \texttt{1}. Its solution
is

\[
u(\phi) \;=\; \frac{GM/L^{2}}{\alpha^{2}} \;+\; B\,\cos\!\bigl(\alpha(\phi - \phi_{0})\bigr)
\;=\; \frac{1}{p\,\alpha^{2}}\,\Bigl[\,1 + e'\cos\bigl(\alpha(\phi - \phi_{0})\bigr)\,\Bigr], \tag{21}
\]

with rescaled eccentricity \texttt{e\textquotesingle{}\ =\ B\ p\ α²}.
The residual \texttt{d²u/dφ²\ +\ α²\ u\ −\ GM/L²} evaluated on (21)
gives \texttt{0} in Wolfram\footnote{Wolfram MCP verification:
  substitute the ansatz (21) into the framework Binet equation (20); the
  residual evaluates to \texttt{0}.}.

The perihelion-to-perihelion period in \texttt{φ} is now \texttt{2π/α},
not \texttt{2π}. Thus the advance (or recession) per Newtonian orbit
(\texttt{2π} in \texttt{φ}) is

\[
\Delta\phi^{\text{framework}}_{\text{per orbit}}
\;=\; \frac{2\pi}{\alpha} \;-\; 2\pi
\;=\; 2\pi\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (GM)^{2}/(c^{2}\,L^{2})}} \;-\; 1\right). \tag{22}
\]

Using the same small parameter as in GR,

\[
\epsilon \;=\; \frac{GM}{c^{2}\,p} \;=\; \frac{(GM)^{2}}{c^{2}\,L^{2}}, \tag{23}
\]

(the second equality follows from \texttt{p\ =\ L²/GM}), we
Taylor-expand

\[
\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\epsilon}} \;-\; 1
\;=\; -\,\frac{\epsilon}{2} \;+\; \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}). \tag{24}
\]

It follows that, to first order,

\[
\Delta\phi^{\text{framework}}_{\text{per orbit}}
\;\approx\; -\,\pi\,\epsilon
\;=\; -\,\frac{\pi\,GM}{c^{2}\,p}
\;=\; -\,\frac{\pi\,GM}{c^{2}\,a\,(1-e^{2})}. \tag{25}
\]

The Taylor expansion (24) is verified symbolically in
Wolfram\footnote{Wolfram MCP verification:
  \texttt{Series{[}2π/√(1\ +\ x)\ −\ 2π,\ \{x,\ 0,\ 2\}{]}} returns
  \texttt{−π\ x\ +\ 3π\ x²/4\ +\ O(x³)}.}: \texttt{2π/√(1\ +\ x)\ −\ 2π}
is \texttt{−π\ x\ +\ 3π\ x²/4\ +\ O(x³)}, with leading term
\texttt{−π\ x} at \texttt{x\ =\ GM/(c²\ p)}.

For Mercury, using the same parameters as in §3,

\[
\Delta\phi^{\text{framework}}_{\text{Mercury}}
\;=\; -\,\frac{\pi\,GM_{\odot}}{c^{2}\,p}
\;\times\; 415.2\;\text{orbits/century}
\;\times\; \frac{180 \cdot 3600}{\pi}
\;=\; -\,7.17\;\text{arcsec/century}. \tag{26}
\]

The numerical evaluation gives \texttt{−7.1653}
arcsec/century\footnote{Wolfram MCP verification:
  \texttt{−π\ GM/(c²\ p)\ ·\ 415.2\ ·\ (180\ ·\ 3600/π)} evaluates to
  \texttt{−7.1653} arcsec/c.}.

\begin{center}\rule{0.5\linewidth}{0.5pt}\end{center}

\hypertarget{the-structural-identity-ux3b4ux3c6_framework-ux3b4ux3c6_gr--16}{%
\subsection{\texorpdfstring{§5. The structural identity
\texttt{Δφ\_framework\ /\ Δφ\_GR\ =\ -1/6}}{§5. The structural identity Δφ\_framework / Δφ\_GR = -1/6}}\label{the-structural-identity-ux3b4ux3c6_framework-ux3b4ux3c6_gr--16}}

Comparing (14) and (25) directly,

\[
\frac{\Delta\phi^{\text{framework}}}{\Delta\phi^{\text{GR}}}
\;=\; \frac{-\,\pi\,GM/(c^{2}\,p)}{+\,6\pi\,GM/(c^{2}\,p)}
\;=\; -\,\frac{1}{6}\qquad\text{(exactly, not approximately).} \tag{27}
\]

Both predictions scale as \texttt{GM/(c²\ p)} per orbit; the
dimensionless small parameter is the same in (14) and (25). Thus the
ratio (27) is independent of the orbital parameters of the test body. It
follows that the \texttt{−1/6} is a structural identity of the two
formulations, not a numerical fit. The same ratio holds for Mercury, for
Venus, for Earth, for S2 around Sgr A*, and for any binary pulsar. The
ratio also carries an opposite-sign signature; the framework's force law
makes the perihelion regress against the orbital motion, while general
relativity makes it advance.

\hypertarget{why-the-structural-difference-the-missing-five-sixths-and-the-sign-flip}{%
\subsection{§6. Why the structural difference --- the missing
five-sixths and the sign
flip}\label{why-the-structural-difference-the-missing-five-sixths-and-the-sign-flip}}

The Schwarzschild Binet equation (7) carries a \texttt{3GM\ u²/c²}
correction. The framework's Binet equation (20) carries a
\texttt{−(GM)²\ u/(c²\ L²)} correction. The structural origin of the
difference is as follows.

General relativity contributes both from the time dilation in the
geodesic equation and from the spatial-metric curvature in the
Schwarzschild line element

\[
ds^{2} \;=\; -\,(1 - r_{g}/r)\,(c\,dt)^{2} \;+\; (1 - r_{g}/r)^{-1}\,dr^{2} \;+\; r^{2}\,d\Omega^{2}. \tag{28}
\]

The time-dilation contribution alone produces a perihelion advance
smaller than the full GR result. The curved-space radial coordinate,
expressed in the \texttt{(1\ −\ r\_g/r)\^{}\{−1\}} factor on
\texttt{dr²}, supplies the rest. In the parameterised post-Newtonian
(PPN) language, the perihelion advance per orbit is

\[
\Delta\phi \;=\; \frac{2 + 2\gamma - \beta}{3}\;\cdot\;\frac{2\pi\,GM}{c^{2}\,p}, \tag{29}
\]

where \texttt{γ} and \texttt{β} are the PPN parameters of the
metric\footnote{C. M. Will, \emph{Theory and Experiment in Gravitational
  Physics}, 2nd ed. (Cambridge University Press, 2018), §7.3, gives the
  PPN perihelion-advance formula (29).}. In general relativity
\texttt{γ\ =\ β\ =\ 1}, and (29) reduces to
\texttt{(2\ +\ 2\ −\ 1)/3\ =\ 1}, giving \texttt{6π\ GM/(c²\ p)} per
orbit, in agreement with (14).

The framework's force law (17) is, by contrast, a force-law-only
modification of Newtonian gravity. It modifies the time dynamics (how
fast \texttt{r} and \texttt{φ} change in time) but does not curve space;
there is no modified \texttt{dr²} coefficient, and the radial coordinate
stays flat. In PPN language this corresponds to \texttt{γ\ =\ 0} and a
value of \texttt{β} determined by the way the \texttt{V²/(2mc²)} kernel
in (16) translates to the static-isotropic metric expansion. With
\texttt{γ\ =\ 0} and an appropriate \texttt{β}, the PPN coefficient
\texttt{(2\ +\ 2γ\ −\ β)/3} can go negative --- which is what the
explicit Binet calculation in §4 reproduces.

We observe that this is the \texttt{1/6} factor of modified-Newtonian
gravity without spatial-metric curvature. It is a known signature in the
PPN literature; any modification that adds a relativistic correction to
the central force but leaves the spatial metric flat produces a
perihelion shift that is some fraction of the GR result. For the
specific form (17), the fraction is \texttt{−1/6}.

The open question, raised for the author's verdict, is whether the
framework's intended content includes a spatial-metric curvature
component beyond Eq. (17), or whether (17) is taken to be the complete
framework prediction for gravity. Under the latter reading, the
framework's Mercury prediction is the \texttt{−7.17} arcsec/century
value computed via Binet in §4, and the framework does not match the
observed \texttt{≈\ +43} arcsec/century. Under the former reading, the
missing five-sixths might be supplied by a curved-space companion to
(17) that the \emph{Relativistic Newtonian Theory} paper does not write
out explicitly.

performed\footnote{Wolfram MCP numerical evaluation: with

  \texttt{pMerc\ =\ aMerc\ ·\ (1\ −\ eMerc²)}, the expressions
  \texttt{dGR\ =\ 6π\ GMsun/(cc²\ pMerc)\ ·\ orbitsPerCentury\ ·\ radToArcsec}
  and
  \texttt{dFramework\ =\ −π\ GMsun/(cc²\ pMerc)\ ·\ orbitsPerCentury\ ·\ radToArcsec}
  give \texttt{+42.99} and \texttt{−7.17} respectively, with
  \texttt{dFramework/dGR\ =\ −1/6} to six significant figures.} with the

\texttt{a\ =\ 5.7909\ ×\ 10¹⁰} m, \texttt{e\ =\ 0.20563},

\[
\Delta\phi^{\text{GR}}_{\text{Mercury}}
\;=\; \frac{6\pi\,GM_{\odot}}{c^{2}\,p}\;\cdot\;\text{(orbits/century)}\;\cdot\;\text{(rad → arcsec)}

\subsection{§8. Honest scope --- what this derivation does and does not
show}\label{honest-scope-what-this-derivation-does-and-does-not-show}}

The derivation in §3 and §4 shows the following. The classical
Binet's-equation method handles both Newton's \texttt{−GM/r²} and the
framework's \texttt{−(GM/r²)(1\ −\ GM/(c²r))} with the same machinery.
For Schwarzschild GR, the standard \texttt{6π\ GM/(c²\ p)} result drops
out by solving the perturbed Binet equation to first order in
\texttt{GM/(c²\ p)}. For the framework's force law (17), taken as the
complete force prescription, the perihelion advance per orbit is exactly
\texttt{−π\ GM/(c²\ p)}, that is, \texttt{−1/6} of the GR result. The
minus sign comes from the framework's perturbation being linear in
\texttt{u}, not quadratic --- a structural difference from the
Schwarzschild \texttt{3GM\ u²/c²} term. Numerically for Mercury, the
figures are \texttt{+42.99} arcsec/century in GR and \texttt{−7.17}
arcsec/century in the framework.

The derivation does not show the following. It does not establish that
the framework as a whole is incompatible with the Mercury observation;
the calculation tests one specific gravitational extension, namely (17)
read as the full framework prediction for gravity. If the framework's
intended content includes a curved-space companion to (17) (a
\texttt{(1\ +\ GM/(c²r))} factor on the radial metric, or an
equivalent), the result changes. The derivation does not show that the
framework's proper-time apparatus is wrong; the proper-time kernel (16)
is verified at the algebraic level by Wolfram MCP\footnote{Pull request

  The latter is the original apsidal-angle calculation showing the
  \texttt{−1/6} result, now reproduced via Binet's equation in §4 of the
  present report.}, and the question here is only what observable
prediction (17) produces. Finally, the derivation does not litigate the
foundations of general relativity; the Schwarzschild calculation in §3
is the textbook result, used here as a known calibration target.
